Your career data is spread across a dozen sites. Stats on [Elite Prospects](https://www.eliteprospects.com/), the backbone, with over [1.2 million player profiles](https://www.eliteprospects.com/about) going back to 1999. Highlights on YouTube. Recaps and features on team sites and local news. A roster page with your headshot. Your Instagram has the rest. It is all public, all findable by anyone with a browser, and none of it is consolidated.

So if you're trying to understand a player, you're opening tabs. If you're the player, you probably don't know half of what's out there about you.

This note is a walk through the system I built to fix that. It takes a name and a date of birth at signup, reads the messy public data, decides what is actually about that player, and assembles one page while they watch. Here is what it uses, how it scores candidates, how I test it, what it costs, and where I think it goes next.

## The pipeline

A name and a date of birth are the whole input. Everything else is discovered. The run is a plain async composition of small, independently testable modules, wrapped by an [Inngest](https://www.inngest.com/) worker that adds retries and idempotency. Stages:

**Discover.** A search provider turns the name into candidate URLs. [Serper](https://serper.dev/) is the default; Brave is a one-env-var rollback. Both sit behind a circuit breaker: three failures in a row trips it, and calls fail fast for an hour instead of hammering a provider that is already down.

**Parse.** An Elite Prospects page is built for browsers, not machines. The parser pulls structured fields (team history, position, nationality, stats by season) from messy HTML and embedded JSON-LD. Reading unstructured text into clean structure is the one thing LLMs are genuinely reliable at.

**Match.** This is the dangerous step. Picking the wrong EP profile writes a stranger's career onto your page. A judge decides which candidate (if any) is you, and how sure it is. More on the scoring below.

**Collapse.** A typical EP profile has fifteen or more season rows. U16, U18, J20, senior, affiliate stints, call-ups. Most of that is noise on a profile page. The collapse step groups rows into career chapters. Three seasons in one club's youth system become one line. A two-game call-up elsewhere gets dropped. The output reads like a timeline, not a spreadsheet.

**Enrich.** The same search-and-judge pattern runs for YouTube, news, and Instagram, each with its own rules. Highlights, channels, articles, and a verified handle get attached.

**Consolidate.** Everything lands on one page, and every section links back to where it came from.

## How candidates get scored

Nothing gets auto-attached on a name match alone. Same-name doppelgangers are the failure mode I design against the hardest. Each source weighs evidence differently.

**Elite Prospects** runs on a confidence tier: `high`, `medium`, or `low`. The tier decides what happens to the candidate.

- `high` applies automatically.
- `medium` is shown to you in the signup walkthrough to confirm or reject.
- `low` is dropped.

The signals that move the tier are a single-candidate hit (if search returns exactly one EP profile for your name, that is strong on its own), an exact date-of-birth match, and a fuzzy name match that tolerates transliteration, accents, hyphenation, middle names, and Jr/Sr. One rule sits outside the model and cannot be overruled by it: if a candidate's name is within edit distance three of your name and the date of birth matches exactly, it never drops below `medium`. A deterministic check guards the most expensive mistake.

**YouTube** is rule-based, not a model call. A channel goes `high` (auto-link) only if it is an auto-generated Topic channel or its description contains your team name or birth year. Everything else is `low` and surfaced as a suggestion, capped at three so the walkthrough stays short.

**Instagram** is search plus scrape plus judge. The finder runs `"<name>" hockey site:instagram.com`, scrapes the candidate profiles, and a judge reads the bio. A bio that names your team or a team from your EP history is a strong yes. A different sport, profession, or geography is a confident no. Name similarity alone is never enough to link.

**News** is a batch. URLs that are obviously stat pages or social handle pages get filtered out first as a cost gate. What survives goes to one judge call that disambiguates every article at once against your parsed team history, then a liveness check drops dead links and a dedupe step drops Facebook cross-posts of the same Instagram content.

One design rule shows up in every source: when unsure, the bias is to reject loudly rather than guess. A wrong attachment on a public profile is worse than a missing one.

## The reasoning prompts

The rules above live in the system prompts. They are written like instructions to a careful assistant, not like code. Two condensed examples (em-dashes removed, trimmed for length).

The Elite Prospects matcher:

```text
You are matching a player's signup info (name plus optional DOB) against
EliteProspects profiles. The wrong match writes someone else's career onto
a stranger's public profile, so be cautious.

A single candidate returned from search is a strong signal: it means the
search found exactly one EP profile for this name. Treat that as almost
certainly the right player unless the DOB actively contradicts. Even then,
surface it as "medium" so the user can confirm or reject in the walkthrough.

Treat name match fuzzily: transliteration, accents, hyphenation, middle
names, Jr/Sr. Only use "low" when no name matches, or when every candidate
in a multi-candidate set has a conflicting DOB.
```

The news disambiguator:

```text
You are deciding which articles in a batch are about a specific hockey
player. Same-name disambiguation is the central concern.

If an article describes the player on a team that appears nowhere in their
team_history (and does not match the signup team), it is about a different
person with the same name. Return is_about_this_player=false.

Old articles are NOT automatically rejected. Career profiles want junior
awards, college commitments, and historical milestones. Judge each article
on whether the team and league align with team_history, regardless of date.
```

### How it responds, and what gets stored

The judges never reply in prose. Each call asks for structured output against a strict schema, so the model has to answer in fixed fields: a `confidence` tier, an index or boolean for the pick, and a one-sentence `reason`. A free-text "I think this is probably him" is not a valid response. The schema is the contract, and the result is rejected if it doesn't fit.

Where that response lands depends on the tier, and nothing destructive happens on the model's say-so:

- `low` writes nothing. No row, no trace on the profile.
- `medium` writes a single candidate row and stops. The page is untouched until you confirm it in the walkthrough.
- `high` applies. It writes the child rows (career journey, stats, social links) and fills in player fields like position and birth year, but only into blanks. A value you already set is never overwritten.

Every applied row is stamped with the run that produced it, so if you later reject a candidate, the exact rows it wrote can be pulled back out cleanly. And the `reason` rides along on every candidate, so when something does land wrong, the explanation is sitting right there in the row instead of lost inside a model call.

## Keeping it accurate

The judges are the brain, but the harness around them is what makes it trustworthy. Every module takes its model call and its data layer as injected dependencies, so the logic can be tested with fakes and no network. There are over fifty test files. Most encode a specific past mistake as a fixture: the player whose two-game call-up should be dropped, the doppelganger article that must not attach, the EP page whose stale social links should be ignored. End-to-end tests run real signups through a browser.

One file is a standing guard against regressions I have already paid for once: blocked metadata endpoints, no error details leaking to clients, no auth check removed from an endpoint that exists to stop LLM cost abuse. Some of those tests grep the source for forbidden patterns. If I need to change what they assert, I have to change the test in the same commit, on purpose.

## What it costs to run

The whole pipeline runs on Gemini 2.5 Flash, which is cheap enough to spend per player without thinking about it. Cost control is structural, not a spend alarm after the fact.

- The Apify scrape budget is a hard per-run cap (ten calls), and any stage that hits it fails open with an empty result instead of looping.
- The news judge estimates batch cost up front and truncates the article list before the call rather than discovering the overage afterward.
- Every stage emits an event to an append-only log, including a `cost.cap_hit` event when a guard trips, so a run is fully traceable after the fact.

Add it up and a full run costs roughly five cents per player. A run makes about six Gemini Flash calls (EP match, the career-chapter collapse, the YouTube and news judges, the Instagram classifier and profile judge), and at Flash's token prices those land near three cents total. The handful of searches add well under a cent. The capped Instagram scrapes add a cent or two. That is an estimate from the per-run call and token counts, not a billed invoice, but the shape is clear: this is a nickel-per-player problem, not a dollar one.

## Where this goes

Right now the intelligence runs once, at signup, and then the page is yours to edit. The more interesting version is continuous.

Picture the profile wired to a standing reasoning layer instead of a one-time script. It watches your sources. A new game posts to Elite Prospects and your stats update themselves. A local paper writes you up and the article appears, headlined and sourced, waiting for your nod. A highlight goes online and threads into the right season. You ask it, in plain language, to feature the playoff run and quiet down the midseason slump, and the page rearranges. It tells you what is being said about you and where, the way an agent would, except it never sleeps and it reads everything.

That is the real shift. Your online presence as a player stops being a dozen accounts you half-maintain and becomes one surface, managed by something that understands hockey, answers to you, and keeps itself current. The scattered-data problem is not really a search problem. It is a coordination problem, and coordination is what a reasoning layer is for.

## Where I'm at

The pipeline works today. A player types their name, confirms a date of birth, and watches the profile build. It is not finished. The EP parser still meets edge cases. The news judge is a touch conservative. The YouTube finder misses channels that don't use a full name.

But the approach holds, the cost is pennies, and the goal hasn't moved. One link. One page. Everything about your hockey career in one place, and eventually, keeping itself that way.
