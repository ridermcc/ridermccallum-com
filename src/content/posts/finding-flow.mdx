*Written on a park bench in Kichijoji.*

I've recently seen a collection of Devin Cooley post-game interviews. He speaks about playing unconscious. That when he's having lots of fun and is emotionally involved, it's his unconscious mind that plays. He claims the conscious mind is no good at playing goalie, and that trusting his unconscious goaltending really allows the actual work he's put in to shine through, instead of consciously reacting to circumstances throughout a game.

At the point of writing, Cooley has a 1.5 GAA, a 0.955 SV%, and is 4-0-0 on a rebuilding Calgary Flames hockey team.

Playing unconscious is the goal when attempting to enter flow, but getting there consistently is something only great athletes can achieve. It becomes a natural and instant process that's baked into their athletic identity.

This season my awareness has been focused on this topic. I wanted to understand why I had it some games (effortlessly played unconscious), and why in other games I was reactionary and feared downside risk.

I started by dissecting every moment the switch flipped, every memory I could resurrect where I played fully uninterrupted in flow. Every game.

I found that in all these moments, and collections of consecutive moments, it boiled down to four main components to entering an unconscious state, among a few extra factors that either enhanced or accelerated the experience.

## 1. Bringing conscious awareness and understanding to what a moment is and how it can be interacted with

In reflection of all my unconscious moments, this is not a mantra I recited, but rather an embrace. A deep breath. An appreciation of sensory inputs and what created them in that moment. It put me in sync and prepared me to ride one moment to the next without interruption, just by an awareness of the present.

It didn't take in-depth scientific or philosophical research into what a moment is defined by, or at what interval of time a human brain takes to perceive and align with current events. We are human. This alignment with the present happens naturally when the past and the future are surrendered from conscious thought.

## 2. I had a clear understanding of what needed to be achieved

A vague request from the universe makes it very hard for you to become in sync with it to achieve a desired outcome. It becomes a dance with the moment, and if you aren't aligned with why you chose to dance, it loses its harmony and beauty.

## 3. I believed I could dance

In every moment I knew in my heart and in my soul I wielded the capabilities to achieve my desired outcome over a designated interval of time. I knew I put in the work. And the work lived in the subconscious (unconscious) mind.

To surrender to the subconscious mind was the only way to express myself fully. To play my best game. To dance.

But with a present conscious, a clear outcome to achieve, and belief in my abilities, one last thing needed to align.

## 4. A dissolution of my ego and a surrendering of my perceived self-image

The ego, the idea of the self, lives in the conscious mind. If I were to play to the expectations of others, I would be placing external judgement on my self-image above my desire to achieve the goals I set out to accomplish. Fear of external judgement makes it impossible to play in an unconscious state.

In all my favorite memories of playing the game in the flow state, I had nothing but a relationship with the moment and all that happened inside of it. It was a dance I had no conscious control over. My reactions and anticipations were driven by my subconscious hockey mind that had trained thousands and thousands of hours for those exact moments. And it was in those moments I let it play.

For many, myself included, completely dropping all external pressures and expectations is an impossibly difficult task. But it is in the moments we free ourselves from the grip our conscious mind has on us that we let ourselves dance.
